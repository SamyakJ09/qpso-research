\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

This section presents the empirical findings of our comparative study, organized into three subsections addressing convergence behavior, dimensional scaling, and statistical significance. We conclude with a broader discussion of the practical implications. All results are drawn from 50 independent runs per algorithm--benchmark--dimensionality combination using the mathematical randomness mode of QPSO; the circuit-based randomness modes are reserved for future work, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Convergence Analysis}
\label{subsec:convergence}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-curves} presents the convergence curves for Classical PSO and QPSO across all six benchmark functions at $n = 10$. Each curve depicts the median global best fitness over 50 independent runs, with the shaded region indicating the inter-quartile range.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{convergence_grid.png}
\caption{Median convergence trajectories (solid lines) with inter-quartile ranges (shaded bands) over 50 independent runs, for all six benchmark functions at $n = 10$. The vertical axis is logarithmic; trajectories are clamped at $10^{-10}$ for display. Classical PSO (tomato) reaches lower residuals on the smoother landscapes, while the two methods are closely matched on the rugged Rastrigin, Rosenbrock, and Griewank functions at this dimensionality.}
\label{fig:convergence-curves}
\end{figure}

On the unimodal Sphere function, both algorithms converge reliably toward the global optimum, but their trajectories differ in character. QPSO descends more steeply in the early and middle iterations, whereas Classical PSO exhibits a slower initial descent followed by a rapid terminal collapse to near machine precision. At $n = 10$, Classical PSO reaches a mean final fitness of $4.5 \times 10^{-25}$, far below QPSO's $3.6 \times 10^{-12}$. This gap reflects a structural property of the two update rules: Classical PSO's velocity-driven contraction allows unbounded refinement once the swarm localizes, while QPSO's probabilistic position sampling retains a residual stochastic ``jitter'' that establishes a precision floor near $10^{-12}$. The smooth, convex Sphere landscape therefore favors the exploitation-oriented dynamics of Classical PSO.

The multimodal benchmark functions reveal a more balanced picture. On Rastrigin, the two convergence curves are largely overlapping, and the difference in median final fitness is not statistically significant at any dimensionality (Section~\ref{subsec:significance}). However, the curves conceal an important distinction: Classical PSO more frequently arrests at the first ring of local minima, whereas QPSO's quantum-inspired update more often tunnels into the global basin. We quantify this escape behavior directly in Section~\ref{subsec:significance}. On the Ackley function, both algorithms descend steadily, with QPSO again showing a steeper mid-optimization slope; at moderate dimensionality Classical PSO ultimately attains a lower residual, but this ordering reverses at $n = 20$.

The Rosenbrock function, with its narrow curved valley, is navigated comparably by both algorithms. After a shared rapid descent into the valley, progress along the ridge is slow for both methods, and their median trajectories remain closely interleaved (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-rosenbrock}). At $n = 10$ the final-fitness difference is not statistically significant; at $n = 20$, however, Classical PSO attains a modestly but significantly lower residual, reflecting the value of its momentum-driven refinement along the lengthening valley floor.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{convergence_rosenbrock.png}
\caption{Median convergence with inter-quartile range on the Rosenbrock function at $n = 20$ over 50 runs. After a shared rapid descent into the valley, both algorithms make slow progress along the curved ridge; Classical PSO (tomato) settles at a modestly lower final residual, a difference that is statistically significant at this dimensionality (Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon}).}
\label{fig:convergence-rosenbrock}
\end{figure}

On Griewank, both algorithms reach the low-fitness regime quickly, after which the product term's many shallow local minima cause both to plateau between $10^{-2}$ and $10^{-1}$. The median trajectories cross repeatedly, and no consistent advantage emerges. The Schwefel function, with its deceptive landscape and geometrically distant second-best minimum, is the hardest of the suite: neither algorithm approaches the global optimum in ten dimensions, and both stall at high residual error. Notably, QPSO's wider sampling distribution sustains exploration for longer, which translates into a lower final residual at the highest dimensionality, as detailed below.

The dominant pattern is a \emph{dimensional crossover}. In low dimensions ($n = 2$, and frequently $n = 5$), Classical PSO is the stronger optimizer on the smooth and moderately rugged landscapes: on Sphere, Ackley, and Rosenbrock it drives the fitness to near machine precision, several orders of magnitude below QPSO's stochastic floor. This confirms that when the search space is small enough for adequate coverage, the aggressive velocity-based exploitation of Classical PSO is difficult to beat, and QPSO's inherent position noise becomes a liability rather than an asset.

As dimensionality grows, the ordering shifts. At $n = 20$, QPSO overtakes Classical PSO on Sphere ($5.9 \times 10^{-12}$ vs.\ $2.6 \times 10^{-9}$), Ackley ($7.7 \times 10^{-7}$ vs.\ $2.1 \times 10^{-1}$), and Schwefel ($1.99 \times 10^{3}$ vs.\ $2.24 \times 10^{3}$). The Ackley case is the most striking: Classical PSO's swarm collapses prematurely and stalls at a fitness five orders of magnitude worse than QPSO's. The mechanism is consistent across these functions---in high-dimensional spaces the velocity of Classical PSO decays before the swarm has adequately explored, trapping it in a suboptimal basin, whereas QPSO's position update samples from a distribution centered on the weighted mean best and thereby maintains exploratory reach deeper into the run. QPSO's advantage is thus not a blanket superiority on multimodal functions, but a specific resistance to premature convergence that manifests at high dimensionality.

On the deceptive Schwefel function, QPSO achieves the lower mean at every dimensionality, and the advantage is statistically significant at $n = 2$ and $n = 20$. This is the one benchmark on which the sustained-exploration property of QPSO yields a consistent, if modest, edge across the dimensional range. On Rastrigin and Griewank the two algorithms are statistically indistinguishable in mean final fitness across most dimensionalities, motivating the escape-rate analysis of the following subsection.

Across the 24 benchmark--dimensionality combinations, Classical PSO achieves a statistically significant advantage in 9 cases, QPSO in 6 cases, and no significant difference is observed in the remaining 9 cases. The mean-fitness comparison, however, understates QPSO's behavior on the archetypal multimodal benchmark. On Rastrigin, the Wilcoxon test reports no significant difference at any dimensionality, yet this metric is dominated by run-to-run variance on such a rugged landscape. At $n = 5$, QPSO escapes into the global basin in 41 of 50 runs, compared with 27 of 50 for Classical PSO---a difference that is statistically significant under Fisher's exact test ($p = 0.005$), despite the corresponding mean-fitness difference being non-significant. This is a direct empirical demonstration of the quantum-tunneling analogy that motivates QPSO: its probabilistic position update escapes local optima more reliably than velocity-based movement. At $n = 2$ the problem is trivial for both algorithms (both escape in every run), while at $n \geq 10$ the Rastrigin landscape defeats both methods within the 500-iteration budget, leaving no separation to detect. The escape advantage is therefore confined to the intermediate regime in which the problem is hard enough to trap Classical PSO but still tractable for QPSO---a finding we return to as a target for future algorithmic improvement in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{subsec:discussion}

The empirical evidence supports a more nuanced conclusion than a simple ranking of the two algorithms. Classical PSO and QPSO occupy complementary regions of the problem space, and the deciding factor is the balance between exploitation and sustained exploration demanded by the landscape and its dimensionality.

Classical PSO excels when the search space is small or the landscape is smooth. Its velocity-based update, with a decaying inertia weight, contracts the swarm efficiently once a promising basin is located, achieving refinement to near machine precision on the Sphere, low-dimensional Ackley, and low-dimensional Rosenbrock functions. QPSO cannot match this precision because its position update samples from a probability distribution whose width does not vanish, imposing a stochastic floor on the attainable fitness. On easy problems, therefore, the quantum-inspired mechanism offers no benefit and a measurable cost.

QPSO's strength is its resistance to premature convergence. Because each particle's next position is drawn from a distribution centered on the weighted mean best rather than propelled by a decaying velocity, the swarm retains exploratory reach even in the later stages of the search. This property is largely irrelevant in low dimensions, where Classical PSO explores adequately regardless, but it becomes decisive at $n = 20$, where Classical PSO's swarm collapses before covering the space. The dramatic reversal on Ackley at $n = 20$---five orders of magnitude in QPSO's favor---is the clearest illustration, complemented by QPSO's consistent edge on the deceptive Schwefel landscape and its significantly higher local-minimum escape rate on Rastrigin at $n = 5$.

These findings qualify the common expectation that quantum-behaved swarms uniformly dominate on multimodal problems. In our controlled, paired-seed comparison the advantage is conditional: it emerges specifically where premature convergence is the failure mode, i.e.\ at high dimensionality and on deceptive landscapes. The stagnation-detection and scatter mechanism, shared by both algorithms, mitigates but does not eliminate premature convergence in Classical PSO, and its velocity-perturbation recovery is evidently less effective in high-dimensional spaces than the intrinsic stochasticity of the QPSO update. Practically, this suggests that QPSO is the preferable choice when the problem is high-dimensional or suspected to be deceptive, whereas Classical PSO remains superior when high-precision solutions to smooth or low-dimensional problems are required. It also points to a concrete avenue for improvement, pursued in future work: coupling QPSO's exploratory update with a precision-oriented local-refinement phase to recover the machine-precision convergence that classical PSO attains on easy landscapes without sacrificing the high-dimensional robustness that is QPSO's principal advantage.
